\begin{abstract}
When a persistent latent regime drives both a treatment and an outcome, a standard remedy is to infer the regime with a hidden Markov model and condition on its posterior. We show that this soft conditioning leaves a bias with a closed form, an omitted-variable-bias identity in the residual state variance. The bias is governed by the \emph{residual state variance} $v$ left in the posterior and by two regime shifts: one in the treatment ($\Delta_T$) and one in the outcome ($\Delta_g$). It equals $\Delta_T\Delta_g v/(\Delta_T^2 v+\sigma^2)$, where $\sigma^2$ is the exogenous treatment variance. The bias is non-monotone in confounding strength, so the conditioning of the residualized treatment (the usual Gram-matrix $\lambda_{\min}$) is not a monotone predictor of reliability. The quantity $v$ is estimable by a plug-in for calibrated posteriors, with an error bounded by the calibration error (which itself requires labels or a validation set), leaving $\Delta_g$ as the single unidentified parameter, which yields a one-parameter sensitivity analysis and a robustness value. In learned-HMM simulations with dependent data, linear and boosted nuisances, and two or three regimes, the plug-in law predicts the realized bias with a median relative error of about 5\% for two regimes (about 10\% for three). The step from known to EM-fitted parameters is assumed (Assumption~\ref{as:post}), not proved. It also reproduces the bias of a difficulty-frontier benchmark, and it shows that a regime-aware coupled estimator is no better than regime fixed effects. On four Indian air-quality networks, the Delhi estimate is robust to residual regime confounding while the other cities are inconclusive.
\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION}
Observational time series are often shaped by a persistent hidden regime: a boundary-layer state in atmospheric data, a macro regime in finance, an operating mode in a plant. If the regime moves both the treatment and the outcome, adjusting for observed covariates $X_t$ does not identify the effect. A common remedy is to fit a hidden Markov model (HMM) to auxiliary proxies $Z_t$ and then condition on the regime posterior $\hg_t$, either as a hard label or as a soft probability, inside a double machine learning (DML) estimator \citep{chernozhukov2018double}. The practical question is how far the result can be trusted when the posterior is imperfect.

Two quantities are commonly used to answer it: the uncertainty of the posterior (its entropy or $L^1$ error $\varepsilon_\gamma$) and the conditioning of the score Jacobian, whose smallest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\min}$ measures how much treatment variation is left after residualization. Heuristically, error then scales like $\varepsilon_\gamma/\lambda_{\min}$. We show that this heuristic is wrong in a specific, checkable way, and we replace it with a closed-form law (exact for the learner-defined residual variance, Remark~\ref{rem:learner}).

\textbf{Contributions.}
(1) \emph{A residual-state bias law} (Thm.~\ref{thm:law}, Cor.~\ref{cor:hump}). For soft conditioning on a posterior computed from the proxy sequence, $\hth-\theta=\Delta_T\Delta_g v/(\Delta_T^2v+\sigma^2)+O_P(N^{-1/2})$, with a matrix version for $K>2$ regimes. This is proved for a posterior that is a fixed function of the proxies; for EM-fitted parameters and dependent data it holds under Assumption~\ref{as:post}, which we state and test numerically but do not prove.
(2) \emph{Non-monotone difficulty.} The bias is hump-shaped in $\Delta_T$ with peak $|\Delta_g|\sqrt v/(2\sigma)$, whereas the residual treatment variance (the scalar analogue of $\lambda_{\min}(\hat J)$) rises monotonically. Refining the conditioning information can only reduce the bias (Prop.~\ref{prop:info}); soft posteriors beat hard regime labels.
(3) \emph{Observability and sensitivity analysis} (Prop.~\ref{prop:calib}, Sec.~\ref{sec:sens}). $v$ is estimated by the posterior Gini impurity, with error at most $\sqrt{CE}+CE$ in the posterior's calibration error ($CE$ requires the true state or a labelled validation set, so on real data calibration must be argued, not measured). Only $\Delta_g$ is unidentified, giving a robustness value that says how large a hidden outcome shift must be to explain an estimate away.
(4) \emph{Evidence with strong baselines} (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp}). The plug-in law matches simulations to a median relative error of about 5\% ($K{=}2$; 9.8\% for $K{=}3$), explains the bias of our own difficulty benchmark, exposes an experimental pitfall (nuisances that omit the regime), and is applied to four Indian cities.

\textbf{Relation to prior work.} Mathematically the law is an omitted-variable-bias (OVB) identity with a partially observed confounder \citep{greenland1980effect,cinelli2020making,chernozhukov2022long}; the hump follows from the same algebra that underlies bias amplification \citep{pearl2012class,ding2017instrumental}. We do not claim these ingredients as new. What we add is (i) the identification of the residual variance with the calibrated HMM posterior, valid for learned posteriors and dependent data under purged cross-fitting; (ii) the consequence that the Gram-matrix conditioning, a standard DML diagnostic, is not a monotone predictor of reliability here; and (iii) a calibration-based, nearly fully observable sensitivity analysis for latent Markov regimes. Posterior-based adjustment is known to fail without identification \citep{wang2019blessings,damour2019multi}; our law quantifies how it fails. Latent-state and proximal approaches \citep{miao2018identifying,tchetgen2020introduction} target identification, which we do not assume. HMMs follow \citet{hamilton1989new,cappe2005inference}; dynamic DML is in \citet{lewis2020double}.

\section{SETTING AND ESTIMATOR}\label{sec:setting}
Let $S_t\in\{0,\dots,K{-}1\}$ be a latent persistent regime with indicator vector $D_t=(\mathbb 1\{S_t=k\})_{k=1}^{K-1}$. We observe a treatment $T_t$, an outcome $Y_t$, controls $X_t$, and proxies $Z_t$ that depend on $S_t$. We posit
\begin{align}
T_t &= m(X_t)+a^\top D_t+V_t, \label{eq:T}\\
Y_t &= \theta\,T_t+g(X_t)+b^\top D_t+U_t, \label{eq:Y}
\end{align}
where $a\in\mathbb R^{K-1}$ and $b\in\mathbb R^{K-1}$ are the regime shifts of the treatment and the outcome ($\Delta_T$ and $\Delta_g$ when $K=2$), $\theta$ is the causal effect, and $(V_t,U_t)$ are exogenous noises (assumptions below). The regime is confounding when $a\neq0$ and $b\neq0$.

\textbf{Soft conditioning.} Let $\hg_t=\hg_t(Z_{1:N})\in\Delta^{K-1}$ be any posterior computed from the proxies, for example the smoothing or filtering distribution of a Gaussian HMM whose parameters are learned by EM on the same sample. With features $F_t=(X_t,\hg_t)$ and purged block cross-fitting with an embargo of $\tau$ steps, let $\tilde T_t=T_t-\hat m_T(F_t)$ and $\tilde Y_t=Y_t-\hat m_Y(F_t)$ be cross-fitted residuals and
\begin{equation}
\hth=\textstyle\sum_t\tilde T_t\tilde Y_t\big/\sum_t\tilde T_t^2 .
\end{equation}
Hard assignment uses $\mathbb 1\{\arg\max\hg_t\}$ instead of $\hg_t$. The coupled, regime-specific estimator of our earlier work (OR-DML) is analysed in Appendix~\ref{app:ordml}; it does not improve on these baselines.

\section{THE RESIDUAL-STATE BIAS LAW}\label{sec:law}
Let $c_t=\E[D_t\mid F_t]$, $\tilde D_t=D_t-c_t$, $\Sigma_N=N^{-1}\sum_t\tilde D_t\tilde D_t^\top$ (the residual state covariance), and $v=\Sigma_N$ when $K=2$.
\begin{assumption}[Exogenous noise]\label{as:noise}
$V_t$ is independent of $(S,Z,X)$ at all times, $\E V_t=0$, $\Var V_t=\sigma^2>0$. $\E[U_t\mid S,Z,X,V]=0$. Both noise processes have summable autocovariances and bounded $4{+}\delta$ moments.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[Posterior as a local feature]\label{as:post}
The process is stationary and $\alpha$-mixing. $\hg_t$ is a function of $Z_{1:N}$ that depends on $Z_s$ for $|s-t|>\tau$ only through terms of size $\varepsilon_\tau$ in $L^2$ (filter stability), and estimated HMM parameters converge at rate $N^{-1/2}$.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[Nuisance learners]\label{as:nuis}
The cross-fitted learners for $\E[T\mid F]$ and $\E[Y\mid F]$ have $L^2$ errors $r_T,r_Y$ (set $r_h=r_Y+|\theta|r_T$) and the embargo $\tau$ exceeds the mixing time.
\end{assumption}
\begin{theorem}[Residual-state bias law]\label{thm:law}
Under Assumptions~\ref{as:noise}--\ref{as:nuis},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:law}
\hth-\theta=\frac{a^\top\Sigma_N\,b}{a^\top\Sigma_N\,a+\sigma^2}+O_P\!\big(N^{-1/2}+r_Tr_h+\varepsilon_\tau\big),
\end{equation}
with constants depending on $\|a\|,\|b\|$ and $\sigma^{-2}$. In particular $\hth$ is consistent iff $a^\top\Sigma_N b\to0$, and the denominator equals $\Var(\tilde T)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{remark}[$v$ is relative to the learner]\label{rem:learner}
The law is exact for $\Sigma_N$, the residual covariance of the state given the conditioning set \emph{as the nuisance learners can use it}. A linear learner cannot exploit non-linear information in $(X,\hg)$, so $\Sigma_N$ can exceed $\E\Var(S\mid F)$ and the plug-in $\hat v$ can under-predict (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp}, Limitation 5).
\end{remark}
The estimation error of the HMM enters \emph{only through $v$}, because $v$ is defined with respect to the realized posterior. The proof (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}) writes the residuals as $\tilde T=a^\top\tilde D+V-\delta^T$ and $\tilde Y-\theta\tilde T=b^\top\tilde D+U-\delta^h$; all cross terms are centered mixing sums or nuisance products.

\textbf{Intuition.} After partialling out $F_t$, the treatment still contains the part of the regime that the posterior failed to explain, $a^\top\tilde D_t$, and so does the outcome, $b^\top\tilde D_t$. This shared component is a confounder that no longer has a proxy: it contributes covariance $a^\top\Sigma_N b$ to the numerator, while the denominator mixes it with the clean variation $\sigma^2$. A strong treatment shift $a$ makes the unexplained component dominate the treatment residual (so $\Var(\tilde T)$ is large), but then the estimator regresses $\tilde Y$ on a variable that is nearly a copy of $b^\top\tilde D$, and its slope tends to $\theta+b/a$; the bias is free of $\theta$ and shrinks like $b/a$ as $a$ grows. Weak shifts leave little bias to begin with.

\begin{corollary}[Hump, sup bound, information monotonicity]\label{cor:hump}
Let $K=2$ and $B=\Delta_T\Delta_g v/(\Delta_T^2v+\sigma^2)$ with $\lambda:=\Delta_T^2v+\sigma^2=\Var(\tilde T)$.
(i) $|B|\le|\Delta_g|\sqrt v/(2\sigma)$ for all $\Delta_T$, with equality at $|\Delta_T|=\sigma/\sqrt v$.
(ii) $|B|=|\Delta_g|\sqrt v\,\sqrt{\lambda-\sigma^2}/\lambda$, which \emph{increases} in $\lambda$ on $[\sigma^2,2\sigma^2]$ and \emph{decreases} afterwards, while $\lambda$ increases monotonically in $|\Delta_T|$.
(iii) For fixed $(\Delta_T,\Delta_g,\sigma)$, $|B|$ is strictly increasing in $v$.
\end{corollary}
Part (ii) is the correct replacement for the heuristic $\varepsilon_\gamma/\lambda_{\min}$: the numerator $\Delta_T\Delta_g v$ grows with the same confounding strength that inflates $\lambda$, so no function of $(\varepsilon_\gamma,\lambda)$ alone bounds the bias monotonically. The bound (i) decays only as $\sqrt v$, so the worst case is $O(\sqrt{\varepsilon_\gamma})$ even though for fixed $\Delta_T$ the bias is linear in $v$.

\begin{proposition}[Information monotonicity]\label{prop:info}
If $\mathcal F\subseteq\mathcal F'$ are two conditioning sets, then $\Sigma'\preceq\Sigma$, and for $K=2$ $|B'|\le|B|$. In particular, conditioning on the soft posterior never does worse than conditioning on its hard label, and adding informative controls or proxies cannot hurt.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Observable plug-in]\label{prop:calib}
Let $\hat\Sigma=N^{-1}\sum_t[\mathrm{diag}(\hg_t)-\hg_t\hg_t^\top]_{1:,1:}$ (for $K=2$, $\hat v=N^{-1}\sum_t\hg_{t}(1-\hg_{t})$, the posterior Gini impurity) and $CE=\E\|\hg_t-\E[D_t\mid F_t]\|^2$ the $L^2$ calibration error given $F_t$. For $K=2$,
\begin{equation}
\big|\E\hat v-\E v\big|\le\sqrt{CE}+CE,
\end{equation}
so $\hat v=v$ for a calibrated posterior and an over-confident one understates the bias. If $m$ is linear and the posterior is calibrated, the OLS coefficient $\hat a$ of $\hg$ in the regression of $T$ on $(1,X,\hg)$ consistently estimates $a$, and $\sigma^2=\Var(\tilde T)-a^\top\Sigma a$.
\end{proposition}
Heterogeneous effects $\theta(S)$ add the terms $a(\theta_1-\bar\theta)+\bar m(\theta_1-\theta_0)$ to $b$ (Appendix~\ref{app:hetero}), where $\bar m$ is the state-free mean treatment level.

\section{SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS}\label{sec:sens}
By Thm.~\ref{thm:law} and Prop.~\ref{prop:calib}, everything in the bias except $b$ is observable: $\hat a$ from the treatment regression, $\hat\Sigma$ from the posterior, and $\widehat{\Var}(\tilde T)$ from the residuals. For a hypothesized outcome shift $b$ the bias-adjusted estimate is $\hth_{\rm adj}(b)=\hth-\hat a^\top\hat\Sigma b/\widehat{\Var}(\tilde T)$.
\textbf{Robustness value.} The smallest outcome shift that explains the point estimate away is
\begin{equation}
b^*=\frac{|\hth|\,\widehat{\Var}(\tilde T)}{\|\hat\Sigma\hat a\|},
\end{equation}
reported in standard deviations of $\tilde Y$ and benchmarked against the observed regime shift of $Y$ (the coefficient of $\hg$ in an outcome regression), which is not identified but is a natural yardstick. A large $b^*$ means the estimate cannot be overturned by residual regime confounding through this channel; $b^*$ is uninformative when $\hth\approx0$. A block bootstrap resamples contiguous blocks of rows and recomputes $(\hth,\hat v,\hat a,\widehat{\Var}(\tilde T))$.
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Residual-state sensitivity analysis}\label{alg:sens}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Fit an HMM to $Z_{1:N}$; where labels or a validation set permit, check calibration and recalibrate $\hg$ (Prop.~\ref{prop:calib}); otherwise treat $\hat v$ as a lower bound on $v$ if the posterior is over-confident.
\STATE Cross-fit $\hat m_T,\hat m_Y$ on $(X,\hg)$ with purged blocks; compute $\tilde T,\tilde Y,\hth$.
\STATE Compute $\hat\Sigma$ (posterior Gini), $\hat a$ (OLS of $T$ on $(1,X,\hg)$), $\widehat{\Var}(\tilde T)$.
\STATE For a grid of outcome shifts $b$: report $\hth_{\rm adj}(b)$; report $b^*$ in SD of $\tilde Y$ and its bootstrap interval.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{A PRIORI BIAS FROM SENSOR PRIMITIVES}\label{sec:prior}
The law takes $v$ as given. For a continuous latent confounder, $v$ has a closed form in the sensor's information rate and the state's persistence, so the bias of posterior-conditioned DML can be predicted before $(T,Y)$ are seen.
\begin{proposition}[Wiener smoothing error]\label{prop:wiener}
Let $u_t$ be a stationary unit-variance AR(1) with coefficient $\phi$, observed as $Z_t=hu_t+\varepsilon_t$ with $\varepsilon_t\sim N(0,R)$ i.i.d., and let $\iota=h^\top R^{-1}h$. Conditioning on the Kalman-smoothed mean leaves residual variance
\begin{equation}
v=\frac{1-\phi^2}{\sqrt{\bigl(1+\phi^2+\iota(1-\phi^2)\bigr)^2-4\phi^2}},
\end{equation}
and the bias is $\Delta_T\Delta_g v/(\Delta_T^2 v+\sigma^2)$ by Thm.~\ref{thm:law} with $u$ in place of $S$. For $0.02\ll\iota\ll(1-\phi^2)^{-1}$, $v\approx\tfrac12\sqrt{(1-\phi^2)/\iota}$, so the worst-case bias $|\Delta_g|\sqrt v/(2\sigma)$ decays only as $\iota^{-1/4}$; for $\iota\gg(1-\phi^2)^{-1}$, $v\approx1/\iota$ and it decays as $\iota^{-1/2}$.
\end{proposition}
The proof is the Wiener--Kolmogorov smoothing error $v=\int S_u/(1+\iota S_u)\,d\omega/2\pi$ with $S_u(\omega)=(1-\phi^2)/|1-\phi e^{-i\omega}|^2$, evaluated with $\int_0^{2\pi}d\omega/2\pi\,(A-2\phi\cos\omega)^{-1}=(A^2-4\phi^2)^{-1/2}$ \citep{anderson1979optimal}. In 18 simulated configurations ($\phi\in\{0.9,0.97\}$, three sensor strengths, three $\Delta_T$; Table~\ref{tab:kalman}), the closed form matches the Kalman smoother's variance to $2\times10^{-4}$, and the predicted bias matches the DML bias with correlation $0.9987$ and maximum error $0.021$. For a persistent state, halving the worst-case bias therefore needs about $16\times$ the sensor information while $\iota\ll(1-\phi^2)^{-1}$. Discretizing $u$ into a $K$-state HMM posterior acts as a sieve: at $\phi=0.97$, $\iota=2.76$, $\Delta_T=1$, the bias falls from $0.82$ ($K{=}2$) to $0.52$ ($K{=}3$) and $0.39$ ($K{=}4$) and then stops improving ($0.40$ at $K{=}6$), matching the law with $v_K=\Var(u\mid X,\hg_K)$ ($0.78,0.49,0.37,0.38$).

\textbf{Finite-state chains.} For a finite-state chain, $v=\E\Var(S\mid Z_{1:N})$ is the minimum mean-squared smoothing error, so it is at most the linear Wiener error of the rescaled indicator: $v\le\tfrac14\,v_{\rm W}(2\rho-1,\iota)$ with $\iota=\Delta_Z^2 h^\top R^{-1}h$ for emission means $\pm\Delta_Z h$ (a bound, valid for known parameters). It is tight for weak signals (bound/exact ratio $1.04$--$1.4$ for $\Delta_Z\le0.5$ at $\rho=0.9$ only; other $\rho$ not tabulated) and loose for well-separated regimes, where $v$ falls exponentially (exact $v=10^{-4}$ vs.\ bound $0.017$ at $\Delta_Z=2$; App.~\ref{app:ext}). Persistent discrete regimes with informative proxies are therefore far less dangerous than a continuous latent state with the same sensor information, whose bias decays only polynomially.

\textbf{Change-point scaling.} For a persistent two-state chain with Gaussian emissions and Mahalanobis separation $d^2=(\mu_1-\mu_0)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_1-\mu_0)$, residual variance is paid at change points. Let $r=\pi_0\epsilon_0+\pi_1\epsilon_1$ be the switch rate per step. In the isolated-change limit, the posterior of the change location $c$ is $p(k)\propto e^{W_k}$, where $W_{\pm1}\sim N(-d^2/2,d^2)$ is the log-likelihood ratio of moving the change by one site, and only the two sites adjacent to the switch carry non-negligible variance, each contributing $\E[\sigma(W)\sigma(-W)]=\frac{e^{-d^2/8}}{d\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\frac{e^{-w/2}e^{-w^2/2d^2}}{4\cosh^2(w/2)}dw\to\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{e^{-d^2/8}}{d\sqrt{2\pi}}$ (the odd part of $e^{-w/2}$ integrates out and $\int\frac{dw}{4\cosh(w/2)}=\frac\pi2$). Hence $v\approx\sqrt{\pi/2}\;r\,e^{-d^2/8}/d$ as $r\to0$ and $d\to\infty$. This is a derivation sketch, not a proof (we do not control the remainder or the interaction of consecutive switches), and the limit is approached slowly: the exact-HMM constant $v\,d\,e^{d^2/8}/\epsilon$ is $1.63\to1.23$ for $d=4$ and $1.56\to1.23$ for $d=3$ as $\epsilon$ falls from $0.1$ to $10^{-3}$ (\texttt{x16\_hmm\_limit.csv}) (App.~\ref{app:ext}). For the practical range $\rho\in[0.9,0.97]$ an effective constant $\sqrt{8/\pi}$ fits:\begin{equation}v\;\approx\;\sqrt{8/\pi}\;r\,\frac{e^{-d^2/8}}{d}.\end{equation}Against the exact HMM filter (40{,}000 steps, $\rho\in\{0.8,\dots,0.99\}$) this is within $\pm10\%$ for $\rho\le0.95$ and $\pm14\%$ at $\rho=0.97$ ($\Delta_Z\in[0.5,1.5]$, $d^2\in[2.8,25]$), within $0.89$--$1.11$ for a one-dimensional proxy at equal $d^2$ (\texttt{x17\_oned\_check.csv}), and within $0.87$--$1.02$ for asymmetric chains with stationary regime probability as low as $0.1$ (so the relevant quantity is the switch rate $r$, not $\rho$); it drifts up to $+23\%$ at $\rho=0.99$ and is noise-dominated at $\Delta_Z=2$ where $v<10^{-3}$. Plugging this \emph{a priori} $v$ into the law predicts the learned-HMM bias of Sec.~\ref{sec:exp} from sensor primitives alone, but the constant was fitted on exact-HMM $v$ from the same generator, so this is a consistency check, not an out-of-sample test. RMSE over the cells with $\Delta_Z\ge0.8$: smoother with linear nuisances $0.0059$ (data-based $\hat v$: $0.0080$), smoother with boosted nuisances $0.0155$ ($0.0115$), filter with linear nuisances $0.0362$ ($0.0121$); the asymptotic constant $\sqrt{\pi/2}$ gives $0.0150$, $0.0111$, $0.0504$ (\texttt{x18\_apriori\_check.csv}). The a priori law is thus competitive only for smoothing posteriors with linear nuisances; e.g.\ $\Delta_Z{=}0.8$, $\Delta_T{=}8$: predicted $0.230$, observed $0.226$. Follow-up checks show the form carries over but the constants do not: a three-state chain (nearest-pair $d^2$) gave $0.74$--$0.84\times$ the two-state value of $\sum_k\gamma_k(1-\gamma_k)$, and unit-variance $t_5$ proxies (true density in the filter) gave $0.9$--$1.7\times$, increasing with $d^2\in[6,17]$ (\texttt{x13\_switchrate\_ext.csv}). The one-change check (\texttt{x15}) plateaus near $1.11$ rather than $\sqrt{\pi/2}$, which we have not reconciled with the sketch.

\section{BEYOND SENSITIVITY: WHEN CAN $b$ BE LEARNED?}\label{sec:prox}
The law leaves $b$ unidentified. Two routes learn it, and they fail differently.
\textbf{Joint model.} Treat $(Z,T,Y)$ as emissions of the chain (a Markov-switching regression with a common $\theta$) and maximize the likelihood by EM. When the regime model is correct this is essentially unbiased and has the smallest variance in our simulations, even when $Z$ is uninformative (App.~\ref{app:ext}); the price of the modular, two-stage posterior used above is exactly the law. But it inherits the regime model: if the latent confounder is a continuous AR(1) process, a $K{=}2$ joint fit is as biased as posterior-DML (about $+0.8$ on $\theta{=}1$).
\textbf{Proximal route.} Suppose a negative-control outcome $W=cS+\ell T+e$ is available ($c\neq0$; $\ell=0$ means $T$ does not cause $W$), and $Z\perp(U,V,e)\mid S$.
\begin{proposition}[Proximal identification, linear case]\label{prop:prox}
Under the linear model of Sec.~\ref{sec:setting}, the 2SLS regression of $\tilde Y$ on $(\tilde T,\tilde W)$ with instruments $(\tilde T,\tilde\gamma)$, where tildes denote cross-fitted residuals on $X$, converges to $(\theta-\ell b/c,\;b/c)$. Hence $\ell=0$ identifies $\theta$ and $b$ without any model for the latent state, and for $\ell\neq0$ the bias is $-\ell\,\hat\beta_W$, computable for any hypothesized leakage $\ell$.
\end{proposition}
This is the linear special case of proximal causal inference \citep{miao2018identifying,tchetgen2020introduction}; we claim no novelty for the identification argument. What the regime setting adds is (i) the posterior $\hg$ is a valid instrument when $W$ is an external variable, but \emph{not} when $W$ is a lagged outcome, in our checks (see below) and (ii) in a continuous-latent stress test, proximal 2SLS has bias below $0.03$ where posterior-DML and the joint MLE are off by $0.8$--$0.9$ (Table~\ref{tab:ext}). The law still describes the failure: replacing $S$ by the continuous state $u$ and $v$ by $\Var(u\mid X,\hg)$ predicts the bias to within $1.4$--$3.2\%$, slightly under: $0.808$ vs.\ $0.834$ and $0.750$ vs.\ $0.774$ at $\Delta_T{=}1$ (sensor SNR $0.5$, $1$), $0.888$ vs.\ $0.901$ and $0.861$ vs.\ $0.874$ at $\Delta_T{=}2$ (\texttt{x3b\_contlat\_law.csv}). The cost is variance (RMSE $0.02$--$0.16$ vs.\ $0.01$--$0.03$ for the correct joint model) and reliance on a valid negative control; a leaking control ($\ell=0.3$) gives bias $-0.45=-\ell b/c$, as predicted. We also tried the lagged outcome $Y_{t-h}$ as a self-contained negative control ($N{=}20{,}000$, $h{=}4$, $\Delta_T{=}1$, 4 replications; \texttt{x4c\_selfnc\_check.csv}). With iid shocks, proximal 2SLS with the posterior as instrument has mean bias $0.094$ and with raw $Z$ as instrument $0.018$, versus $0.111$ for DML; with AR shocks they are $0.148$ and $0.098$ versus $0.110$. Neither instrument is reliable, so we do not recommend this variant, and we do not claim a mechanism. On the four cities we do not apply the proximal estimator: the available proxies (meteorology) also affect PM$_{2.5}$ directly, so the exclusion restrictions fail.

\textbf{Environment route (no negative control, no joint model).} The law is linear in an observable: in environment $e$, $\theta_e=\theta+b_e k_e$ with $k_e=a_ev_e/\Var(\tilde T_e)$, because $\Var(\tilde T_e)=a_e^2v_e+\sigma_e^2$. If the outcome shift is invariant across environments (or merely independent of $k_e$) while the treatment shift varies, regressing $\hat\theta_e$ on $\hat k_e$ identifies $\theta$ as the intercept, i.e.\ it extrapolates to zero residual confounding. In a simulation with 10 environments of $N{=}1500$ and treatment shifts $\Delta_T\in[0.3,4]$ (12 replications), the intercept has bias $+0.03$ (RMSE $0.04$) for a common $b=3$ and $0.00$ (RMSE $0.05$) for random $b_e$, against $+0.30$ for the average of environment-wise estimates; the slope recovers $b$ ($3.3$, $3.7$). If $b_e$ grows with the treatment shift the intercept is biased ($-0.14$), still smaller than the pooled error ($+0.36$). On the four cities, with calendar months as environments, the route has no leverage: $|k_e|\le0.06$ except in Kolkata (4 months), and monthly $\hat\theta_e$ is too unstable (standard deviations $0.4$--$28$) for the regression to be informative, so we report no real-data estimate from it (\texttt{x11\_envreal.csv}).

\textbf{Setup.} All experiments use \texttt{src/bias\_law/} and share these ingredients unless noted: a persistent Markov chain (stay probability 0.9), 2-d Gaussian proxies with separation $\Delta_Z$, two exogenous controls, $N=3000$, unit-variance noises, $\Delta_g=3$. The HMM is fitted by EM on the same sample (smoother or filter), cross-fitting uses 5 purged blocks with $\tau=24$, and nuisances are linear or gradient boosting. Reported biases are means over 12 replications per cell with $\theta=1$; the law is evaluated with the \emph{observable} plug-ins unless stated.

\textbf{Soft beats hard.} In all 24 $(\Delta_Z,\Delta_T)$ cells the soft posterior has smaller $|{\rm bias}|$ than the hard regime label (median ratio 1.19 where the bias exceeds 0.02), as Prop.~\ref{prop:info} predicts; DML on $X$ alone has mean absolute bias 0.387 versus 0.125 for the soft posterior, 0.142 for the hard label and 0.004 for the oracle state (Fig.~\ref{fig:law}c).

\textbf{Using the law to correct.} Subtracting the plug-in bias $\hat a\,\hat v\,b/\widehat{\Var}(\tilde T)$ with the true $b$ reduces the per-replication mean absolute error from 0.126 to 0.021 (83\%). \subsection{It explains a standard difficulty benchmark}
We rerun our earlier difficulty-frontier DGP ($\theta=(0.75,2.5)$, treatment shift $\Delta_T=4$, outcome offsets 15/50, i.e.\ $\Delta_g=35$), in which ``DML on $X$ only'' has bias $\approx8.5$. \subsection{Why $\lambda_{\min}$ misleads}\label{sec:lam}
A common experiment varies the treatment shift while holding the representation fixed and reads the error against $\lambda_{\min}(\hat J)$. \subsection{Calibration controls the observable bias}
We rescale the posterior logits by a temperature $\tau$ ($\tau<1$ over-confident) and compare the law with $\hat v$ against the realized residual variance (Table~\ref{tab:cal}). A flexible learner is invariant to monotone recalibration, so the realized bias is constant (0.127) while $\hat v$ changes by a factor of 14; the bound $|\hat v-v|\le\sqrt{CE}+CE$ held in 100\% of the 120 runs. An over-confident posterior therefore understates the bias, and the sensitivity analysis should use a recalibrated posterior.

\section{DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS}
We gave an exact, observable-except-one-parameter law for the bias of soft conditioning on a latent Markov posterior. It explains when posterior-based DML fails, why a standard conditioning diagnostic is not monotone in reliability, and how to bound the damage.
\textbf{Practical guidance.} (a) Report $\hat v$, $\hat a$ and the robustness value alongside every posterior-conditioned effect. (b) Do not read $\lambda_{\min}$ of the residualized Gram matrix as a reliability index when a latent confounder may remain; fit nuisances on $(X,\hg)$. (c) Prefer soft posteriors to hard labels, recalibrate over-confident posteriors, and always compare with regime-fixed-effects and proxy-as-control baselines.
\textbf{Limitations.} (1) The law is a probability-limit identity under Assumptions~\ref{as:noise}--\ref{as:nuis}; it is not a finite-sample or uniform-over-HMM-error guarantee, and Assumption~\ref{as:post} (locality of the posterior) is asserted via filter stability, not proved for EM-fitted parameters. (2) Assumption~\ref{as:noise} (treatment noise independent of the proxies) is strong; correlation between the NO$_2$ innovations and meteorology would add terms. (3) The model is linear in the regime indicators and has a single scalar treatment. (4) $b$ is unidentified, so the sensitivity analysis bounds rather than corrects. (5) The plug-in $\hat v$ requires calibration and is defined relative to the nuisance learner class: in a test where $X_1$ informs the regime, the $Z$-only $\hat v$ predicted the bias within 3\% ($0.30$ vs.\ $0.31$), whereas an HMM refit on $(Z,X)$ gave a smaller $\hat v$ and underpredicted it ($0.24$), because a linear nuisance cannot exploit the information the HMM uses. (6) The real-data analysis is illustrative: the proxies partly overlap the controls, lagged pollutants may be mediators, and data-quality issues are material. (7) $v$ is defined relative to the nuisance learner class, so the law is exact only for the learner-defined $v_N$; with the observable plug-in the median relative error is 14\% for boosted nuisances with the smoother, and in the Table-1 DGP the plug-in law deviates by up to 26\% in magnitude (E4). (8) The a priori formulas (Wiener, change-point) are approximations with fitted constants; the change-point derivation is a sketch. (9) The formula is an OVB identity; the new content is its calibrated, dependent-data form and its consequences for DML diagnostics.

\section{PROOFS}\label{app:proofs}
\paragraph{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:law}.}
\emph{Step 0 (exact decomposition).} By Assumption~\ref{as:noise}, $V_t$ is independent of $\mathcal G=\sigma(S,Z,X)$, which contains $F_t$ and $D_t$, so $\E[T_t\mid F_t]=m(X_t)+a^\top c_t$ and $\E[Y_t\mid F_t]=\theta\E[T_t\mid F_t]+g(X_t)+b^\top c_t$. With nuisance errors $\delta^T_t=\hat m_T(F_t)-\E[T_t\mid F_t]$, $\delta^Y_t=\hat m_Y(F_t)-\E[Y_t\mid F_t]$ and $\delta^h=\delta^Y-\theta\delta^T$,
\[
\tilde T_t=a^\top\tilde D_t+V_t-\delta^T_t,\qquad \tilde Y_t-\theta\tilde T_t=b^\top\tilde D_t+U_t-\delta^h_t,
\]
and $\hth-\theta=\sum_t\tilde T_t(\tilde Y_t-\theta\tilde T_t)/\sum_t\tilde T_t^2$ \emph{exactly}.

\emph{Step 1 (main terms).} $N^{-1}\sum(a^\top\tilde D_t)(b^\top\tilde D_t)=a^\top\Sigma_Nb$ and $N^{-1}\sum(a^\top\tilde D_t)^2=a^\top\Sigma_Na$. Also $N^{-1}\sum V_t^2=\sigma^2+O_P(N^{-1/2})$ by the summable-autocovariance condition.

\emph{Step 2 (noise cross terms).} Conditionally on $\mathcal G$, $\sum_tV_tb^\top\tilde D_t$ and $\sum_t a^\top\tilde D_t U_t$ have mean zero (Assumption~\ref{as:noise}) and variance at most $C\sum_t\|\tilde D_t\|^2\le CN$ by summable autocovariances; similarly $\sum_tV_tU_t$ (conditioning on $\mathcal G$ and $V$). Hence each is $O_P(N^{1/2})$.

\emph{Step 3 (nuisance terms).} Within a cross-fitting fold, $\delta_t=\delta(F_t)$ is a fixed function given the training folds, and $\E[\tilde D_t\mid F_t]=0$. The sums $\sum\delta_t\tilde D_t$, $\sum\delta_tV_t$, $\sum\delta_tU_t$ have conditional mean zero up to an $O(\varepsilon_\tau)$ dependence between training and test blocks (embargo plus Assumption~\ref{as:post}) and variance $\le CN\|\delta\|_{L^2}^2$ by the mixing covariance inequality (Davydov), so they are $O_P(rN^{1/2})$. The product term satisfies $|\sum\delta^T_t\delta^h_t|\le N r_Tr_h$ by Cauchy--Schwarz.

\emph{Step 4.} Write the ratio as $(A+e_1)/(B+e_2)$ with $A=a^\top\Sigma_Nb$, $B=a^\top\Sigma_Na+\sigma^2\ge\sigma^2$, and $e_1,e_2=O_P(N^{-1/2}+r_Tr_h+\varepsilon_\tau)$. Then $(A+e_1)/(B+e_2)-A/B=(e_1-(A/B)e_2)/(B+e_2)$ and $|A/B|\le\sqrt{b^\top\Sigma_Nb}/(2\sigma)$ is bounded (Corollary~\ref{cor:hump}(i), matrix form via Cauchy--Schwarz and $x/(x^2+\sigma^2)\le1/(2\sigma)$). $\square$

\paragraph{Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:hump}.}
(i) $a^2v+\sigma^2\ge2|a|\sqrt v\sigma$ (AM--GM), with equality at $|a|=\sigma/\sqrt v$. (ii) $|a|=\sqrt{(\lambda-\sigma^2)/v}$ gives $|B|=|b|v|a|/\lambda$; and $\frac{d}{d\lambda}\sqrt{\lambda-\sigma^2}/\lambda=(2\sigma^2-\lambda)/(2\lambda^2\sqrt{\lambda-\sigma^2})$. (iii) $\partial_v[v/(a^2v+\sigma^2)]=\sigma^2/(a^2v+\sigma^2)^2>0$. $\square$

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:info}.} For $\mathcal F\subseteq\mathcal F'$ the law of total variance gives $\E\Cov(D\mid\mathcal F')\preceq\E\Cov(D\mid\mathcal F)$; hard labels are a measurable function of $\hg$, hence $\sigma(\text{hard})\subseteq\sigma(\hg)$. Part (iii) of Corollary~\ref{cor:hump} finishes the $K=2$ case. $\square$

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:calib}.} With $c=\E[D\mid F]$, $\gamma(1-\gamma)-c(1-c)=(\gamma-c)(1-2c)-(\gamma-c)^2$, so $|\E[\gamma(1-\gamma)-c(1-c)]|\le\E|\gamma-c|+\E(\gamma-c)^2\le\sqrt{CE}+CE$ (Cauchy--Schwarz, $|1-2c|\le1$), and $\E c(1-c)=\E\Var(D\mid F)=v$. If $m$ is linear and $c=\hg$, then $\E[T\mid F]=m(X)+a^\top\hg$ is linear in $(X,\hg)$ and the OLS coefficient is $a$; $\Var(\tilde T)=a^\top\Sigma a+\sigma^2$ is Step~1. $\square$

\paragraph{Learned HMM parameters (what is and is not proved).} Let $\gamma_{\vartheta}$ denote the smoothing posterior under HMM parameters $\vartheta$ and $\vartheta^*$ the pseudo-true value (the Kullback--Leibler minimizer; the truth if the emission model is correct). Two standard facts reduce Assumption~\ref{as:post} to known results: (i) under strong mixing and identifiability the EM/ML estimator $\hat\vartheta$ converges to $\vartheta^*$ up to label permutation \citep{leroux1992maximum,douc2011consistency}; (ii) under strong mixing the posterior $\gamma_{t,\vartheta}$ is continuous in $\vartheta$ uniformly in $t$ and forgets its initial condition geometrically \citep{cappe2005inference}. Together they give $\sup_t\E|\hg_t-\gamma_{t,\vartheta^*}|\to0$, so $\hat v\to v(\vartheta^*)$ and the law holds with $v(\vartheta^*)$. We do \emph{not} prove a rate, nor the cross-fitting independence between $\hat\vartheta$ and the held-out fold (the HMM is fitted on all of $Z_{1:N}$), which we handle only through the embargo; the learned-HMM experiments (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp}) are the evidence that this approximation is benign. Misspecified emissions (e.g.\ a continuous latent) leave $\vartheta^*$ well defined but $v(\vartheta^*)$ large, which the law still predicts (Sec.~\ref{sec:prior}).

\section{HETEROGENEOUS EFFECTS AND MULTIPLE REGIMES}\label{app:hetero}
\paragraph{Heterogeneous effects ($K=2$).} Let $Y_t=\theta(S_t)T_t+g(X_t)+bD_t+U_t$ with $\theta(0)=\theta_0,\theta(1)=\theta_1$, $\bar\theta=\E\theta(S)$, $T_t=\bar m+m_0(X_t)+aD_t+V_t$ with $\E m_0=0$, and assume $\E[m_0(X)\Var(D\mid F)]=0$. Then
\[
\hth\xrightarrow{p}\bar\theta+\frac{a\,v\,[\,b+a(\theta_1-\bar\theta)+\bar m(\theta_1-\theta_0)\,]}{a^2v+\sigma^2}.
\]
\emph{Proof.} $\E[\tilde T\tilde Y]=\E[\tilde T\,\theta(S)T]+ab\,v$; expanding with $\tilde T=a\tilde D+V$ gives $\E[\tilde T\theta(S)T]=a\bar m(\theta_1-\theta_0)v+a^2\theta_1v+\sigma^2\bar\theta$, using $\E[\tilde D\,\theta(S)]=(\theta_1-\theta_0)v$ and $\E[V^2\theta(S)]=\sigma^2\bar\theta$. Subtract $\bar\theta(a^2v+\sigma^2)$. $\square$ The oracle-state estimator ($v=0$) converges to $\bar\theta$.

\paragraph{$K>2$.} Theorem~\ref{thm:law} holds with matrix $\Sigma_N$ and $|B|\le\sqrt{b^\top\Sigma b}/(2\sigma)$; the hump persists along any direction $a=s\,a_0$. Hard-versus-soft monotonicity (Prop.~\ref{prop:info}) holds in the Loewner order but $|B|$ need not be monotone in $\Sigma$ for $K>2$.
