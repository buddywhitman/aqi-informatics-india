\begin{abstract}
When a persistent latent regime drives both a treatment and an outcome, a standard remedy is to infer the regime with a hidden Markov model and condition on its posterior. We show that this soft conditioning leaves a bias with an exact closed form. The bias is governed by the \emph{residual state variance} $v$ left in the posterior and by two regime shifts: one in the treatment ($\Delta_T$) and one in the outcome ($\Delta_g$). It equals $\Delta_T\Delta_g v/(\Delta_T^2 v+\sigma^2)$, where $\sigma^2$ is the exogenous treatment variance. The bias is non-monotone in confounding strength, so the conditioning of the residualized treatment (the usual Gram-matrix $\lambda_{\min}$) is not a monotone predictor of reliability. The quantity $v$ is observable for calibrated posteriors, with an error bounded by the calibration error, leaving $\Delta_g$ as the single unidentified parameter, which yields a one-parameter sensitivity analysis and a robustness value. In learned-HMM simulations with dependent data, linear and boosted nuisances, and two or three regimes, the law predicts the realized bias with a median relative error of about 5\%. It also reproduces the bias of a difficulty-frontier benchmark, and it shows that a regime-aware coupled estimator is no better than regime fixed effects. On four Indian air-quality networks, the Delhi estimate is robust to residual regime confounding while the other cities are inconclusive.
\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION}
Observational time series are often shaped by a persistent hidden regime: a boundary-layer state in atmospheric data, a macro regime in finance, an operating mode in a plant. If the regime moves both the treatment and the outcome, adjusting for observed covariates $X_t$ does not identify the effect. A common remedy is to fit a hidden Markov model (HMM) to auxiliary proxies $Z_t$ and then condition on the regime posterior $\hg_t$, either as a hard label or as a soft probability, inside a double machine learning (DML) estimator \citep{chernozhukov2018double}. The practical question is how far the result can be trusted when the posterior is imperfect.

Two quantities are commonly used to answer it: the uncertainty of the posterior (its entropy or $L^1$ error $\varepsilon_\gamma$) and the conditioning of the score Jacobian, whose smallest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\min}$ measures how much treatment variation is left after residualization. Heuristically, error then scales like $\varepsilon_\gamma/\lambda_{\min}$. We show that this heuristic is wrong in a specific, checkable way, and we replace it with an exact law.

\textbf{Contributions.}
(1) \emph{A residual-state bias law} (Thm.~\ref{thm:law}, Cor.~\ref{cor:hump}). For soft conditioning on any posterior computed from the proxy sequence, with learned parameters and dependent data, $\hth-\theta=\Delta_T\Delta_g v/(\Delta_T^2v+\sigma^2)+O_P(N^{-1/2})$, with a matrix version for $K>2$ regimes.
(2) \emph{Non-monotone difficulty.} The bias is hump-shaped in $\Delta_T$ with peak $|\Delta_g|\sqrt v/(2\sigma)$, whereas the residual treatment variance (the scalar analogue of $\lambda_{\min}(\hat J)$) rises monotonically. Refining the conditioning information can only reduce the bias (Prop.~\ref{prop:info}); soft posteriors beat hard regime labels.
(3) \emph{Observability and sensitivity analysis} (Prop.~\ref{prop:calib}, Sec.~\ref{sec:sens}). $v$ is estimated by the posterior Gini impurity, with error at most $\sqrt{CE}+CE$ in the posterior's calibration error. Only $\Delta_g$ is unidentified, giving a robustness value that says how large a hidden outcome shift must be to explain an estimate away.
(4) \emph{Evidence with strong baselines} (Sec.~\ref{sec:exp}). The law matches simulations to a median relative error of about 5\%, explains the bias of our own difficulty benchmark, exposes an experimental pitfall (nuisances that omit the regime), and is applied to four Indian cities.

\textbf{Relation to prior work.} Mathematically the law is an omitted-variable-bias (OVB) identity with a partially observed confounder \citep{greenland1980effect,cinelli2020making,chernozhukov2022long}; the hump follows from the same algebra that underlies bias amplification \citep{pearl2012class,ding2017instrumental}. We do not claim these ingredients as new. What we add is (i) the identification of the residual variance with the calibrated HMM posterior, valid for learned posteriors and dependent data under purged cross-fitting; (ii) the consequence that the Gram-matrix conditioning, a standard DML diagnostic, is not a monotone predictor of reliability here; and (iii) a calibration-based, nearly fully observable sensitivity analysis for latent Markov regimes. Posterior-based adjustment is known to fail without identification \citep{wang2019blessings,damour2019multi}; our law quantifies how it fails. Latent-state and proximal approaches \citep{miao2018identifying,tchetgen2020introduction} target identification, which we do not assume. HMMs follow \citet{hamilton1989new,cappe2005inference}; dynamic DML is in \citet{lewis2020double}.

\section{SETTING AND ESTIMATOR}\label{sec:setting}
Let $S_t\in\{0,\dots,K{-}1\}$ be a latent persistent regime with indicator vector $D_t=(\mathbb 1\{S_t=k\})_{k=1}^{K-1}$. We observe a treatment $T_t$, an outcome $Y_t$, controls $X_t$, and proxies $Z_t$ that depend on $S_t$. We posit
\begin{align}
T_t &= m(X_t)+a^\top D_t+V_t, \label{eq:T}\\
Y_t &= \theta\,T_t+g(X_t)+b^\top D_t+U_t, \label{eq:Y}
\end{align}
where $a\in\mathbb R^{K-1}$ and $b\in\mathbb R^{K-1}$ are the regime shifts of the treatment and the outcome ($\Delta_T$ and $\Delta_g$ when $K=2$), $\theta$ is the causal effect, and $(V_t,U_t)$ are exogenous noises (assumptions below). The regime is confounding when $a\neq0$ and $b\neq0$.

\textbf{Soft conditioning.} Let $\hg_t=\hg_t(Z_{1:N})\in\Delta^{K-1}$ be any posterior computed from the proxies, for example the smoothing or filtering distribution of a Gaussian HMM whose parameters are learned by EM on the same sample. With features $F_t=(X_t,\hg_t)$ and purged block cross-fitting with an embargo of $\tau$ steps, let $\tilde T_t=T_t-\hat m_T(F_t)$ and $\tilde Y_t=Y_t-\hat m_Y(F_t)$ be cross-fitted residuals and
\begin{equation}
\hth=\textstyle\sum_t\tilde T_t\tilde Y_t\big/\sum_t\tilde T_t^2 .
\end{equation}
Hard assignment uses $\mathbb 1\{\arg\max\hg_t\}$ instead of $\hg_t$. The coupled, regime-specific estimator of our earlier work (OR-DML) is analysed in Appendix~\ref{app:ordml}; it does not improve on these baselines.

\section{THE RESIDUAL-STATE BIAS LAW}\label{sec:law}
Let $c_t=\E[D_t\mid F_t]$, $\tilde D_t=D_t-c_t$, $\Sigma_N=N^{-1}\sum_t\tilde D_t\tilde D_t^\top$ (the residual state covariance), and $v=\Sigma_N$ when $K=2$.
\begin{assumption}[Exogenous noise]\label{as:noise}
$V_t$ is independent of $(S,Z,X)$ at all times, $\E V_t=0$, $\Var V_t=\sigma^2>0$. $\E[U_t\mid S,Z,X,V]=0$. Both noise processes have summable autocovariances and bounded $4{+}\delta$ moments.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[Posterior as a local feature]\label{as:post}
The process is stationary and $\alpha$-mixing. $\hg_t$ is a function of $Z_{1:N}$ that depends on $Z_s$ for $|s-t|>\tau$ only through terms of size $\varepsilon_\tau$ in $L^2$ (filter stability), and estimated HMM parameters converge at rate $N^{-1/2}$.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[Nuisance learners]\label{as:nuis}
The cross-fitted learners for $\E[T\mid F]$ and $\E[Y\mid F]$ have $L^2$ errors $r_T,r_Y$ (set $r_h=r_Y+|\theta|r_T$) and the embargo $\tau$ exceeds the mixing time.
\end{assumption}
\begin{theorem}[Residual-state bias law]\label{thm:law}
Under Assumptions~\ref{as:noise}--\ref{as:nuis},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:law}
\hth-\theta=\frac{a^\top\Sigma_N\,b}{a^\top\Sigma_N\,a+\sigma^2}+O_P\!\big(N^{-1/2}+r_Tr_h+\varepsilon_\tau\big),
\end{equation}
with constants depending on $\|a\|,\|b\|$ and $\sigma^{-2}$. In particular $\hth$ is consistent iff $a^\top\Sigma_N b\to0$, and the denominator equals $\Var(\tilde T)$.
\end{theorem}
The estimation error of the HMM enters \emph{only through $v$}, because $v$ is defined with respect to the realized posterior. The proof (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}) writes the residuals as $\tilde T=a^\top\tilde D+V-\delta^T$ and $\tilde Y-\theta\tilde T=b^\top\tilde D+U-\delta^h$; all cross terms are centered mixing sums or nuisance products.

\textbf{Intuition.} After partialling out $F_t$, the treatment still contains the part of the regime that the posterior failed to explain, $a^\top\tilde D_t$, and so does the outcome, $b^\top\tilde D_t$. This shared component is a confounder that no longer has a proxy: it contributes covariance $a^\top\Sigma_N b$ to the numerator, while the denominator mixes it with the clean variation $\sigma^2$. A strong treatment shift $a$ makes the unexplained component dominate the treatment residual (so $\Var(\tilde T)$ is large), but then the estimator regresses $\tilde Y$ on a variable that is nearly a copy of $b^\top\tilde D$, and its slope tends to $\theta+b/a$; the bias is free of $\theta$ and shrinks like $b/a$ as $a$ grows. Weak shifts leave little bias to begin with.

\begin{corollary}[Hump, sup bound, information monotonicity]\label{cor:hump}
Let $K=2$ and $B=\Delta_T\Delta_g v/(\Delta_T^2v+\sigma^2)$ with $\lambda:=\Delta_T^2v+\sigma^2=\Var(\tilde T)$.
(i) $|B|\le|\Delta_g|\sqrt v/(2\sigma)$ for all $\Delta_T$, with equality at $|\Delta_T|=\sigma/\sqrt v$.
(ii) $|B|=|\Delta_g|\sqrt v\,\sqrt{\lambda-\sigma^2}/\lambda$, which \emph{increases} in $\lambda$ on $[\sigma^2,2\sigma^2]$ and \emph{decreases} afterwards, while $\lambda$ increases monotonically in $|\Delta_T|$.
(iii) For fixed $(\Delta_T,\Delta_g,\sigma)$, $|B|$ is strictly increasing in $v$.
\end{corollary}
Part (ii) is the correct replacement for the heuristic $\varepsilon_\gamma/\lambda_{\min}$: the numerator $\Delta_T\Delta_g v$ grows with the same confounding strength that inflates $\lambda$, so no function of $(\varepsilon_\gamma,\lambda)$ alone bounds the bias monotonically. The bound (i) decays only as $\sqrt v$, so the worst case is $O(\sqrt{\varepsilon_\gamma})$ even though for fixed $\Delta_T$ the bias is linear in $v$.

\begin{proposition}[Information monotonicity]\label{prop:info}
If $\mathcal F\subseteq\mathcal F'$ are two conditioning sets, then $\Sigma'\preceq\Sigma$, and for $K=2$ $|B'|\le|B|$. In particular, conditioning on the soft posterior never does worse than conditioning on its hard label, and adding informative controls or proxies cannot hurt.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Observable plug-in]\label{prop:calib}
Let $\hat\Sigma=N^{-1}\sum_t[\mathrm{diag}(\hg_t)-\hg_t\hg_t^\top]_{1:,1:}$ (for $K=2$, $\hat v=N^{-1}\sum_t\hg_{t}(1-\hg_{t})$, the posterior Gini impurity) and $CE=\E\|\hg_t-\E[D_t\mid F_t]\|^2$ the $L^2$ calibration error given $F_t$. For $K=2$,
\begin{equation}
\big|\E\hat v-\E v\big|\le\sqrt{CE}+CE,
\end{equation}
so $\hat v=v$ for a calibrated posterior and an over-confident one understates the bias. If $m$ is linear and the posterior is calibrated, the OLS coefficient $\hat a$ of $\hg$ in the regression of $T$ on $(1,X,\hg)$ consistently estimates $a$, and $\sigma^2=\Var(\tilde T)-a^\top\Sigma a$.
\end{proposition}
Heterogeneous effects $\theta(S)$ add the terms $a(\theta_1-\bar\theta)+\bar m(\theta_1-\theta_0)$ to $b$ (Appendix~\ref{app:hetero}), where $\bar m$ is the state-free mean treatment level.

\section{SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS}\label{sec:sens}
By Thm.~\ref{thm:law} and Prop.~\ref{prop:calib}, everything in the bias except $b$ is observable: $\hat a$ from the treatment regression, $\hat\Sigma$ from the posterior, and $\widehat{\Var}(\tilde T)$ from the residuals. For a hypothesized outcome shift $b$ the bias-adjusted estimate is $\hth_{\rm adj}(b)=\hth-\hat a^\top\hat\Sigma b/\widehat{\Var}(\tilde T)$.
\textbf{Robustness value.} The smallest outcome shift that explains the point estimate away is
\begin{equation}
b^*=\frac{|\hth|\,\widehat{\Var}(\tilde T)}{\|\hat\Sigma\hat a\|},
\end{equation}
reported in standard deviations of $\tilde Y$ and benchmarked against the observed regime shift of $Y$ (the coefficient of $\hg$ in an outcome regression), which is not identified but is a natural yardstick. A large $b^*$ means the estimate cannot be overturned by residual regime confounding through this channel; $b^*$ is uninformative when $\hth\approx0$. A block bootstrap resamples contiguous blocks of rows and recomputes $(\hth,\hat v,\hat a,\widehat{\Var}(\tilde T))$.
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Residual-state sensitivity analysis}\label{alg:sens}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Fit an HMM to $Z_{1:N}$; recalibrate the posterior $\hg$ if its calibration error is large (Prop.~\ref{prop:calib}).
\STATE Cross-fit $\hat m_T,\hat m_Y$ on $(X,\hg)$ with purged blocks; compute $\tilde T,\tilde Y,\hth$.
\STATE Compute $\hat\Sigma$ (posterior Gini), $\hat a$ (OLS of $T$ on $(1,X,\hg)$), $\widehat{\Var}(\tilde T)$.
\STATE For a grid of outcome shifts $b$: report $\hth_{\rm adj}(b)$; report $b^*$ in SD of $\tilde Y$ and its bootstrap interval.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{EXPERIMENTS}\label{sec:exp}
\textbf{Setup.} All experiments use \texttt{src/bias\_law/} and share these ingredients unless noted: a persistent Markov chain (stay probability 0.9), 2-d Gaussian proxies with separation $\Delta_Z$, two exogenous controls, $N=3000$, unit-variance noises, $\Delta_g=3$. The HMM is fitted by EM on the same sample (smoother or filter), cross-fitting uses 5 purged blocks with $\tau=24$, and nuisances are linear or gradient boosting. Reported biases are means over 12 replications per cell with $\theta=1$; the law is evaluated with the \emph{observable} plug-ins unless stated.

\subsection{The law predicts the bias}
Figure~\ref{fig:law} and Table~\ref{tab:ver} summarize 72 cells for $K=2$ (smoother and filter; linear and boosted nuisances; three proxy qualities $\Delta_Z\in\{0.5,0.8,1.2\}$; eight values of $\Delta_T$ from 0.25 to 32) and 8 cells for $K=3$ (matrix law, four $(a,b)$ configurations including sign changes). The law with the observable plug-in $\hat v$ has correlation 0.996 and RMSE 0.012 with the measured bias, with median relative error 5.2\% on cells with $|{\rm bias}|>0.05$. Using the realized $v_N$ gives 3.5\%, and also estimating $\hat a$ and $\widehat{\Var}(\tilde T)$ from the data gives 14\%. The predicted peak location $\sigma/\sqrt{\hat v}$ and height $|\Delta_g|\sqrt{\hat v}/2\sigma$ match the observed maximum to within the grid (Appendix~\ref{app:exp}).

\textbf{Soft beats hard.} In all 24 $(\Delta_Z,\Delta_T)$ cells the soft posterior has smaller $|{\rm bias}|$ than the hard regime label (median ratio 1.19 where the bias exceeds 0.02), as Prop.~\ref{prop:info} predicts; DML on $X$ alone has mean absolute bias 0.39 versus 0.14 for the soft posterior and 0.01 for the oracle state (Fig.~\ref{fig:law}c).

\textbf{Using the law to correct.} Subtracting the plug-in bias $\hat a\,\hat v\,b/\widehat{\Var}(\tilde T)$ with the true $b$ reduces the per-replication mean absolute error from 0.126 to 0.021 (83\%). Misspecifying $b$ by a factor of 0.5 or 2 gives 0.072 and 0.097 (43\% and 23\% below the uncorrected error). The correction therefore degrades gracefully but is not robust to gross misspecification, which is why we advocate sensitivity analysis rather than point correction.

\subsection{It explains a standard difficulty benchmark}
We rerun our earlier difficulty-frontier DGP ($\theta=(0.75,2.5)$, treatment shift $\Delta_T=4$, outcome offsets 15/50, i.e.\ $\Delta_g=35$), in which ``DML on $X$ only'' has bias $\approx8.5$. Table~\ref{tab:dgp} shows that the \emph{excess} bias of soft conditioning over the oracle-state estimator is predicted by the heterogeneous-effects form of the law (with the realized $v_N$) to within 0.04 at every $\Delta_Z$, from 8.24 at $\Delta_Z=0.2$ to $0.02$ at $\Delta_Z=2$; the observable plug-in $\hat v$ underestimates it by 10--20\%. So the transition between failure and success that this benchmark was built to display is a consequence of $v(\Delta_Z)$, not of a spectral property of the Jacobian.

\textbf{Honest baselines.} With the proxy used as controls, a regime-fixed-effects baseline matches the coupled OR-DML estimator at $\Delta_Z\ge2$ (median absolute bias 0.063 vs.\ 0.072 at $\Delta_Z=2$, 0.046 vs.\ 0.046 at $\Delta_Z=4$) and beats it at $\Delta_Z=1$ (2.64 vs.\ 4.02); the spectral penalty changes OR-DML by less than 1.5\% (Appendix~\ref{app:ordml}). The contribution of this paper is therefore the law and the sensitivity analysis, not a new estimator.

\subsection{Why $\lambda_{\min}$ misleads}\label{sec:lam}
A common experiment varies the treatment shift while holding the representation fixed and reads the error against $\lambda_{\min}(\hat J)$. Table~\ref{tab:lam} (12 replications per cell, boosted nuisances, oracle regime weights) shows the pitfall: if nuisances are fit on $X$ only, the regime shift leaks into $\tilde T$, $\lambda_{\min}$ grows from 0.54 to 14.4, and the error is hump-shaped (5.9, 14.0, 5.7). If the nuisances are fit on $(X,S)$, $\lambda_{\min}$ is flat at 0.51 and the error is 0.06--0.10 for every $\Delta_T$. The hump is the one of Cor.~\ref{cor:hump} with $v=\Var(S)$, whose predicted peak $\sigma/\sqrt v\approx2$ matches the observed peak at 1.5--3. Because $\lambda_{\min}$ moves while the error is flat or falls, $\lambda_{\min}$ does not govern reliability here.

\subsection{Calibration controls the observable bias}
We rescale the posterior logits by a temperature $\tau$ ($\tau<1$ over-confident) and compare the law with $\hat v$ against the realized residual variance (Table~\ref{tab:cal}). A flexible learner is invariant to monotone recalibration, so the realized bias is constant (0.127) while $\hat v$ changes by a factor of 14; the bound $|\hat v-v|\le\sqrt{CE}+CE$ held in 100\% of the 120 runs. An over-confident posterior therefore understates the bias, and the sensitivity analysis should use a recalibrated posterior.

\subsection{Four Indian cities}
We apply the analysis to hourly NO$_2$ (treatment) and PM$_{2.5}$ (outcome) in Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru and Kolkata (Feb 2025--Jun 2026) with the controls and proxies of Appendix~\ref{app:real}. Figure~\ref{fig:cities} and Table~\ref{tab:cities} report the pooled estimate and the robustness value. For Delhi the NO$_2$--PM$_{2.5}$ association is positive and stable, with a robustness value of about 175 standard deviations of $\tilde Y$ (90\% interval 85--605), about 1\,200 times the observed regime shift of PM$_{2.5}$ ($\hat c_Y$, last column): residual confounding through the regime channel cannot plausibly overturn it. Mumbai's and Kolkata's intervals include zero, and Bengaluru's estimate is $\approx0$, so their robustness values are uninformative. Screening runs of $\ge6$ identical NO$_2$ readings changes Mumbai's estimate from 1.9 to 4.9 (885 rows, 21\%), which shows that the estimate is sensitive to data quality; Kolkata's NO$_2$ has 31 distinct values and was nearly constant.

\section{DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS}
We gave an exact, observable-except-one-parameter law for the bias of soft conditioning on a latent Markov posterior. It explains when posterior-based DML fails, why a standard conditioning diagnostic is not monotone in reliability, and how to bound the damage.
\textbf{Practical guidance.} (a) Report $\hat v$, $\hat a$ and the robustness value alongside every posterior-conditioned effect. (b) Do not read $\lambda_{\min}$ of the residualized Gram matrix as a reliability index when a latent confounder may remain; fit nuisances on $(X,\hg)$. (c) Prefer soft posteriors to hard labels, recalibrate over-confident posteriors, and always compare with regime-fixed-effects and proxy-as-control baselines.
\textbf{Limitations.} (1) The law is a probability-limit identity under Assumptions~\ref{as:noise}--\ref{as:nuis}; it is not a finite-sample or uniform-over-HMM-error guarantee, and Assumption~\ref{as:post} (locality of the posterior) is asserted via filter stability, not proved for EM-fitted parameters. (2) Assumption~\ref{as:noise} (treatment noise independent of the proxies) is strong; correlation between the NO$_2$ innovations and meteorology would add terms. (3) The model is linear in the regime indicators and has a single scalar treatment. (4) $b$ is unidentified, so the sensitivity analysis bounds rather than corrects. (5) The plug-in $\hat v$ ignores information carried by $X$ and requires calibration. (6) The real-data analysis is illustrative: the proxies partly overlap the controls, lagged pollutants may be mediators, and data-quality issues are material. (7) The formula is an OVB identity; the new content is its calibrated, dependent-data form and its consequences for DML diagnostics.

\section{PROOFS}\label{app:proofs}
\paragraph{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:law}.}
\emph{Step 0 (exact decomposition).} By Assumption~\ref{as:noise}, $V_t$ is independent of $\mathcal G=\sigma(S,Z,X)$, which contains $F_t$ and $D_t$, so $\E[T_t\mid F_t]=m(X_t)+a^\top c_t$ and $\E[Y_t\mid F_t]=\theta\E[T_t\mid F_t]+g(X_t)+b^\top c_t$. With nuisance errors $\delta^T_t=\hat m_T(F_t)-\E[T_t\mid F_t]$, $\delta^Y_t=\hat m_Y(F_t)-\E[Y_t\mid F_t]$ and $\delta^h=\delta^Y-\theta\delta^T$,
\[
\tilde T_t=a^\top\tilde D_t+V_t-\delta^T_t,\qquad \tilde Y_t-\theta\tilde T_t=b^\top\tilde D_t+U_t-\delta^h_t,
\]
and $\hth-\theta=\sum_t\tilde T_t(\tilde Y_t-\theta\tilde T_t)/\sum_t\tilde T_t^2$ \emph{exactly}.

\emph{Step 1 (main terms).} $N^{-1}\sum(a^\top\tilde D_t)(b^\top\tilde D_t)=a^\top\Sigma_Nb$ and $N^{-1}\sum(a^\top\tilde D_t)^2=a^\top\Sigma_Na$. Also $N^{-1}\sum V_t^2=\sigma^2+O_P(N^{-1/2})$ by the summable-autocovariance condition.

\emph{Step 2 (noise cross terms).} Conditionally on $\mathcal G$, $\sum_tV_tb^\top\tilde D_t$ and $\sum_t a^\top\tilde D_t U_t$ have mean zero (Assumption~\ref{as:noise}) and variance at most $C\sum_t\|\tilde D_t\|^2\le CN$ by summable autocovariances; similarly $\sum_tV_tU_t$ (conditioning on $\mathcal G$ and $V$). Hence each is $O_P(N^{1/2})$.

\emph{Step 3 (nuisance terms).} Within a cross-fitting fold, $\delta_t=\delta(F_t)$ is a fixed function given the training folds, and $\E[\tilde D_t\mid F_t]=0$. The sums $\sum\delta_t\tilde D_t$, $\sum\delta_tV_t$, $\sum\delta_tU_t$ have conditional mean zero up to an $O(\varepsilon_\tau)$ dependence between training and test blocks (embargo plus Assumption~\ref{as:post}) and variance $\le CN\|\delta\|_{L^2}^2$ by the mixing covariance inequality (Davydov), so they are $O_P(rN^{1/2})$. The product term satisfies $|\sum\delta^T_t\delta^h_t|\le N r_Tr_h$ by Cauchy--Schwarz.

\emph{Step 4.} Write the ratio as $(A+e_1)/(B+e_2)$ with $A=a^\top\Sigma_Nb$, $B=a^\top\Sigma_Na+\sigma^2\ge\sigma^2$, and $e_1,e_2=O_P(N^{-1/2}+r_Tr_h+\varepsilon_\tau)$. Then $(A+e_1)/(B+e_2)-A/B=(e_1-(A/B)e_2)/(B+e_2)$ and $|A/B|\le\sqrt{b^\top\Sigma_Nb}/(2\sigma)$ is bounded (Corollary~\ref{cor:hump}(i), matrix form via Cauchy--Schwarz and $x/(x^2+\sigma^2)\le1/(2\sigma)$). $\square$

\paragraph{Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:hump}.}
(i) $a^2v+\sigma^2\ge2|a|\sqrt v\sigma$ (AM--GM), with equality at $|a|=\sigma/\sqrt v$. (ii) $|a|=\sqrt{(\lambda-\sigma^2)/v}$ gives $|B|=|b|v|a|/\lambda$; and $\frac{d}{d\lambda}\sqrt{\lambda-\sigma^2}/\lambda=(2\sigma^2-\lambda)/(2\lambda^2\sqrt{\lambda-\sigma^2})$. (iii) $\partial_v[v/(a^2v+\sigma^2)]=\sigma^2/(a^2v+\sigma^2)^2>0$. $\square$

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:info}.} For $\mathcal F\subseteq\mathcal F'$ the law of total variance gives $\E\Cov(D\mid\mathcal F')\preceq\E\Cov(D\mid\mathcal F)$; hard labels are a measurable function of $\hg$, hence $\sigma(\text{hard})\subseteq\sigma(\hg)$. Part (iii) of Corollary~\ref{cor:hump} finishes the $K=2$ case. $\square$

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:calib}.} With $c=\E[D\mid F]$, $\gamma(1-\gamma)-c(1-c)=(\gamma-c)(1-2c)-(\gamma-c)^2$, so $|\E[\gamma(1-\gamma)-c(1-c)]|\le\E|\gamma-c|+\E(\gamma-c)^2\le\sqrt{CE}+CE$ (Cauchy--Schwarz, $|1-2c|\le1$), and $\E c(1-c)=\E\Var(D\mid F)=v$. If $m$ is linear and $c=\hg$, then $\E[T\mid F]=m(X)+a^\top\hg$ is linear in $(X,\hg)$ and the OLS coefficient is $a$; $\Var(\tilde T)=a^\top\Sigma a+\sigma^2$ is Step~1. $\square$

\section{HETEROGENEOUS EFFECTS AND MULTIPLE REGIMES}\label{app:hetero}
\paragraph{Heterogeneous effects ($K=2$).} Let $Y_t=\theta(S_t)T_t+g(X_t)+bD_t+U_t$ with $\theta(0)=\theta_0,\theta(1)=\theta_1$, $\bar\theta=\E\theta(S)$, $T_t=\bar m+m_0(X_t)+aD_t+V_t$ with $\E m_0=0$, and assume $\E[m_0(X)\Var(D\mid F)]=0$. Then
\[
\hth\xrightarrow{p}\bar\theta+\frac{a\,v\,[\,b+a(\theta_1-\bar\theta)+\bar m(\theta_1-\theta_0)\,]}{a^2v+\sigma^2}.
\]
\emph{Proof.} $\E[\tilde T\tilde Y]=\E[\tilde T\,\theta(S)T]+ab\,v$; expanding with $\tilde T=a\tilde D+V$ gives $\E[\tilde T\theta(S)T]=a\bar m(\theta_1-\theta_0)v+a^2\theta_1v+\sigma^2\bar\theta$, using $\E[\tilde D\,\theta(S)]=(\theta_1-\theta_0)v$ and $\E[V^2\theta(S)]=\sigma^2\bar\theta$. Subtract $\bar\theta(a^2v+\sigma^2)$. $\square$ The oracle-state estimator ($v=0$) converges to $\bar\theta$.

\paragraph{$K>2$.} Theorem~\ref{thm:law} holds with matrix $\Sigma_N$ and $|B|\le\sqrt{b^\top\Sigma b}/(2\sigma)$; the hump persists along any direction $a=s\,a_0$. Hard-versus-soft monotonicity (Prop.~\ref{prop:info}) holds in the Loewner order but $|B|$ need not be monotone in $\Sigma$ for $K>2$.
