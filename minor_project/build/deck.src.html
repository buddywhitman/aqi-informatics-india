The question

Does cutting NO₂ cut PM₂.₅?

A forecast cannot answer this. The atmosphere has a hidden state, such as stagnation or ventilation, that raises both pollutants together.

We never observe that state. We only see weather proxies.

{{SVG:dag}}
Traffic-related NO2 and PM2.5 rise together in stagnant air. The regime is a confounder that persists for hours to days and is seen only via meteorology.

Why standard methods fail

Standard DML is off by 5×, at every proxy quality.

Adding more observed controls can make the bias worse, not better.

Theorem 1: omitted-regime bias. The denominator shrinks as controls explain more of the treatment, while the confounding numerator does not move (bias amplification).
Approach

Infer the state, then estimate carefully.

{{SVG:pipeline}}

Hidden Markov model gives a probability of each regime at each hour.

Cross-fitting skips neighbouring hours so time dependence cannot leak.

A regularized coupled score gives one effect per regime.

OR-DML: Gaussian HMM, purged block cross-fitting, posterior-adjusted nuisances, spectral regularization, HAC inference.
Key discovery

A worse state estimate can look better.

We call it phantom causal resolution: error rises while the usual conditioning check improves, up to 70×.

Left: error is a hump in proxy error. Middle: smallest eigenvalue of the generated-state Gram matrix grows with proxy error. Right: 82% of learned-HMM runs lie above the identity line.
The rules: why it fails

Two laws explain the failure.

Omitted-regime bias

$$\hat\theta_{\rm naive}\to\sum_k\omega_k\theta_k^*+\mathcal B_{\rm het}+\mathcal B_{\rm conf}$$

Controls alone cannot see the hidden regime. More controls can make the bias worse.

State-confusion law

$$\lambda_-=\tfrac12\sigma^2(1-\varepsilon_\gamma)^2+\tfrac18\varepsilon_\gamma^2\Delta^2$$

Conditioning improves as the state estimate gets worse. Matches 4,900 runs.

Theorem 1 and Proposition 1 with Corollary 1 in the report (sections 3.2 and 5.3). The weights omega are variance shares of each regime in the residual treatment; B_het and B_conf are the heterogeneity and confounding terms. In the second law, epsilon is the posterior error, sigma squared is treatment noise, Delta is the regime gap in treatment mean. When epsilon exceeds 8 sigma squared over (Delta squared plus 4 sigma squared), the generated state looks better conditioned than the true one.
The rules: what we build from them

Three rules shape the estimator.

Orthogonality

$$\partial_{(m,\mu)}\,\mathbb E\,\psi_k=0$$

Condition nuisances on the posterior: first-order errors drop out for every posterior.

Error bound

$$\|\hat\theta-\theta^*\|\lesssim\frac{\varepsilon_\gamma}{\lambda_{\min}(J)}$$

State error and nuisance error add; neither hides the other.

Residual-state bias

$$B=\frac{a\,b\,v}{a^{2}v+\sigma^{2}}$$

State error × treatment shift × outcome shift, over a variance the shift inflates.

Lemma 1, Theorem 3 and Theorem 4 in the report (sections 5.1, 5.2, 5.4). a and b are the regime shifts in treatment and outcome, v is the leftover state variance the posterior cannot explain, sigma squared is treatment noise. Peak bias at a equals sigma over root v. Full proofs in Annexure G.

Conclusions

Two rules survive.

Design

Use the posterior as an adjustment feature. Use it as a weight only on the target.

Reporting

State the leftover state variance, the regime shifts, a robustness value, simple baselines, and aligned placebos.

Trust is not a function of posterior confidence and conditioning alone.
Limits and next steps

What we do not claim.

Limits

Every method fails at weak proxy separation. City results are associations. Inference theory excludes full-sample smoothing.

Next

Open-source package. Policy-shock designs for emission claims. Re-test the dual-factor idea of ambiguity times conditioning.

Be direct about limits. Future work is hypotheses, not findings.
