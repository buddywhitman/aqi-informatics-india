## 2.1 Causal inference from observational data

**Potential outcomes and identification.** The potential-outcomes view of causal effects goes back to Rubin (1974), and Holland (1986) formalised its logic and its fundamental limitation: only one potential outcome is ever observed per unit. Effects are identified from observational data under *unconfoundedness* (no unmeasured confounding given covariates) and overlap. Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983) showed that conditioning on the propensity score suffices for adjustment, and Hahn (1998) established the semiparametric efficiency bound for average treatment effects that later estimators target. Imbens (2004) reviews the nonparametric estimators that follow from these conditions, and Angrist et al. (1996) give the complementary instrumental-variable route when unconfoundedness is not credible. The graphical alternative expresses the same assumptions as a diagram, with identification read from the structure of paths (Pearl (1995)); a textbook treatment of the potential-outcomes approach is Imbens and Rubin, 2015.

**Continuous treatments and the partially linear model.** With a continuous treatment and many confounders, adjustment is done by regression, by weighting, or by a combination. The partially linear model $Y = \theta T + g(X) + U$ with $T = m(X) + V$ is the workhorse: the effect $\theta$ is the coefficient on the *residual* of $T$ after removing what the controls explain. This residualisation device is due to Robinson (1988), who obtained $\sqrt N$-consistent estimation of $\theta$ with nonparametric nuisance estimates. It is the starting point of the estimator used in this project.

**Observational emulation.** Hernán and Robins (2016) formalise the idea that an observational analysis should be designed to emulate a hypothetical randomised "target trial" (eligibility, treatment strategy, time zero, follow-up). We borrow the discipline, not the machinery: Chapter 3 states the structural model and the target estimand before any estimator is fitted. Rubin (2008) argues the same point from the design side: the analysis plan should be fixed before outcomes are examined.

## 2.2 Double / Debiased Machine Learning

Chernozhukov et al. (2018) showed that if the nuisance functions $m(X)=\mathbb{E}[T\mid X]$ and $\mu(X)=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]$ are estimated by flexible machine learning, then the residual-on-residual estimator of $\theta$ is still $\sqrt N$-consistent and asymptotically normal, provided two ingredients are present.

* **Neyman orthogonality.** The estimating equation is insensitive, to first order, to errors in the nuisances. First-order nuisance mistakes then contribute only second-order terms, which are negligible when nuisance rates are $o(N^{-1/4})$.
* **Cross-fitting.** Nuisances are estimated on data not used to evaluate the equation, removing own-observation overfitting bias.

**Antecedents.** Orthogonal scores are older than the DML label. Newey (1994) derived the influence-function calculus that gives the asymptotic variance of semiparametric estimators; Robins et al. (1994) constructed estimators that stay consistent when either the outcome model or the treatment model is correct, and Bang and Robins (2005) gave the doubly robust formulation that is the template for orthogonal estimating equations. Belloni et al. (2014) and Belloni et al. (2014) showed that post-selection inference for treatment effects with many controls requires exactly this type of orthogonality, and Chernozhukov et al. (2017) previewed the DML construction for treatment effects. Kennedy (2016) gives a readable account of the empirical-process arguments behind cross-fitting, and Hines et al. (2022) explain how efficient influence functions yield orthogonal scores in practice.

**Extensions.** Automatic debiasing (Chernozhukov, Newey and Singh, 2022) removes the need to derive the correction term by hand, with neural and forest versions in Chernozhukov et al. (2022). Locally robust estimation for general functionals is treated by Chernozhukov et al. (2022). Orthogonal statistical learning (Foster and Syrgkanis, 2023) and quasi-oracle heterogeneous effects (Nie and Wager, 2021) extend the idea to conditional effects; Semenova and Chernozhukov (2021) give debiased inference on conditional average effects, and Kennedy (2023) derives optimal doubly robust estimation of heterogeneous effects. Deep networks are shown to be admissible nuisance learners in Farrell et al. (2021). Tree ensembles targeted at heterogeneity are developed in Athey and Imbens (2016), Wager and Athey (2018) and Athey et al. (2019); meta-learner decompositions are compared in Künzel et al. (2019). DML has also been adapted to difference-in-differences designs (Chang (2020)) and to conditional effects in high dimensions (Fan et al. (2022)).

**What is common to these results.** They are stated for independent data and *observed* confounders. The two assumptions that this project relaxes, independence and observability of the confounder, are exactly the assumptions on which the guarantees above rest.

## 2.3 Dependent data: time-series DML

**Why independence matters.** Time series break the independence assumption. Random $K$-fold cross-fitting leaks information across neighbouring observations, because training and evaluation folds are correlated. Validity of cross-validation for dependent data is a long-standing concern in forecasting: Stone (1974) introduced cross-validatory assessment for independent data; Racine (2000) showed that for dependent data one must remove a block of observations around the evaluation point (*hv-block* validation); Bergmeir and Benítez (2012) and Bergmeir et al. (2018) document when plain $K$-fold remains acceptable for autoregressive models and when it does not. In financial machine learning, *purging* and *embargo* of training observations adjacent to the evaluation block are standard remedies (López de Prado, 2018). Our implementation uses contiguous blocks with an embargo of at least $C\log N$ steps, matching the mixing conditions needed for asymptotic theory.

**Mixing and concentration.** The theoretical analysis of learning from dependent data is built on strong-mixing ($\alpha$-, $\beta$-) coefficients (Bradley (2005)). Yu (1994) proved the blocking technique that reduces dependent sequences to independent blocks, and McDonald et al. (2015) showed how $\beta$-mixing coefficients can be estimated in practice. Matrix concentration for dependent sequences (Banna, Merlevède and Youssef, 2016) is the tool behind the Gram-matrix statements in Chapter 5.

**Variance estimation under serial dependence.** Heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) standard errors (Newey and West (1987); Andrews (1991)) and block resampling (Kunsch (1989); Politis and Romano (1994)) are the classical remedies; our uncertainty statements use the block bootstrap and HAC bandwidth rules from this literature.

**Causal inference for time series.** Granger (1969) defined predictive causality, and Sugihara et al. (2012) proposed convergent cross-mapping for coupled dynamical systems; both address *predictability*, not effects of interventions. Runge et al. (2019) combine conditional-independence tests with momentary-conditional-independence to recover lagged causal links, and Runge et al. (2019) review the use of such methods in the Earth sciences, a setting that includes air quality. Marginal structural models (Robins et al. (2000)) handle time-varying treatment under sequential unconfoundedness. Bojinov and Shephard (2019) define causal estimands and randomisation tests for single-unit time-series experiments. Intervention analysis for one treated unit uses synthetic controls (Abadie et al. (2010)) or Bayesian structural time-series counterfactuals (Brodersen et al. (2015)); these need an untreated comparison unit or a pre-period that is stable, which a continuous exposure such as hourly NO$_2$ does not provide. Lewis and Syrgkanis (2020) treat dynamic treatment effects with g-estimation; Ciganovic et al. (2026) develop DML for time series. All of these works assume the confounders are observed.

## 2.4 Latent confounders and regime models

Several lines handle unobserved confounding.

* **Representation approaches.** Sequential deconfounders and the time-series deconfounder learn a latent confounder with neural encoders (Bica et al., 2020; Hatt and Feuerriegel, 2021). The cross-sectional ancestors are the substitute-confounder idea of "multiple causes" and its deep-latent-variable relatives (Louizos et al. (2017); Ranganath and Perotte (2018); Veitch et al. (2019); Shi et al. (2019)). Posterior-based adjustment is known to fail without identification (Wang and Blei, 2019; D'Amour, 2019), and Ogburn et al. (2019) sharpen the objection to the substitute-confounder argument. Our results sharpen this caution: *representation quality and downstream reliability are different objects*.
* **Bounds under unobserved confounding.** When identification is lost, effects can still be bounded: Kallus et al. (2019) derive interval estimates of individual-level effects, and Manski (1990) gave the original nonparametric bounds. We do not bound; we characterise the bias that a *specific* generated surrogate leaves behind and report how large a violation would overturn the estimate.
* **Proximal causal inference.** Proxies of an unmeasured confounder identify effects under completeness conditions (Miao, Geng and Tchetgen Tchetgen, 2018; Tchetgen Tchetgen et al., 2020; Mastouri et al., 2021; Cui et al., 2024). The target there is identification; we study what a *plug-in* posterior leaves behind. See Section 2.5.
* **Regime-switching models.** Hamilton (1989) introduced Markov regime switching; Hamilton (1990) gave the likelihood analysis and Chib (1998) Bayesian estimation of multiple change-point models. The hidden-Markov machinery itself is older: the forward-backward and maximisation steps of Baum and Petrie (1966) and Baum et al. (1970), the EM algorithm of Dempster et al. (1977) and the tutorial of Rabiner (1989). The identifiability and asymptotics of hidden Markov models are classical (Leroux, 1992; Bickel, Ritov and Rydén, 1998; Douc, Moulines and Rydén, 2004; Allman, Matias and Rhodes, 2009); Anandkumar et al. (2014) show that spectral methods identify and estimate latent-variable models from low-order moments, and Gassiat et al. (2016) study inference for finite-state hidden Markov models without parametric emissions. Causal inference for latent Markov models has been studied with the parametric g-formula (Clouth et al., 2025).
* **Stepwise latent-model analysis.** Estimating a latent-class model first and using its posteriors in a second stage is common, and classification error already motivates bias-adjusted corrections (Di Mari, Oberski and Vermunt, 2016). Vermunt (2010) give the three-step approaches with modal and proportional assignment, and Bakk et al. (2013) correct the standard errors of the second step. These adjust for *misclassification of a discrete class*; our setting differs in that the posterior enters a continuous-treatment residual equation, where the error is not a symmetric misclassification. We test the natural two-stage heuristic, not joint likelihood methods.

## 2.5 Proxies and negative controls

A proxy is a variable that carries information about an unmeasured confounder without being a cause of treatment or outcome; a *negative control* is a proxy known a priori to be unaffected by the treatment (negative-control exposure or outcome). Lipsitch et al. (2010) introduced negative controls as a diagnostic: a "treatment effect" on an outcome that cannot be affected is evidence of confounding. Shi et al. (2020) review the epidemiological use. Formal identification with two proxies (one on each side) is the proximal framework of Miao et al. (2018) and Tchetgen Tchetgen et al. (2020), with doubly negative controls analysed by Shi et al. (2020), panel-data versions by Deaner (2018), and minimax estimation by Kallus et al. (2021). The operational lesson for this project is the diagnostic one: our *placebo* tests (Section 8.3) are negative controls in the sense of Lipsitch et al., 2010, and the threats section (Chapter 11) states which proxies would be needed for point identification, which the data do not provide.

## 2.6 Omitted-variable bias, amplification and overlap

Our bias laws are omitted-variable identities (Greenland, 1980; Cinelli and Hazlett, 2020; Chernozhukov et al., 2022), and their non-monotonicity is the algebra of *bias amplification*: conditioning on variables that explain the treatment but not the confounder can magnify the residual confounding (Pearl, 2012; Ding, VanderWeele and Robins, 2017). The phenomenon was first noted as a warning against placing instruments in propensity scores (Bhattacharya and Vogt (2007)), analysed with simulations in epidemiology (Myers et al. (2011); Pearl (2011)) and in political science (Middleton et al. (2016)), and its consequence for matching is discussed by Wooldridge (2016). We claim neither identity as new. What is new is their calibrated, dependent-data and regime-coupled form.

**Overlap and irregular identification.** Weak overlap is diagnosed in the literature by Gram-matrix or propensity diagnostics (Crump et al., 2009; D'Amour et al., 2021). Its inferential consequences are developed by Khan and Tamer (2010), who show that near-violations of overlap slow convergence and make standard confidence intervals unreliable, by Ma and Wang (2020) and Heiler and Kazak (2021), who give inference that remains valid with many extreme propensity scores, and by Li et al. (2018), who replace inverse weights with overlap weights that are bounded by construction. Stuart (2010) reviews matching and balance checks as design diagnostics. A central finding here is that such conditioning diagnostics can move in the wrong direction when the state is generated.

## 2.7 Sensitivity analysis

When identification cannot be certified, the standard response is a sensitivity analysis that reports how strong an unmeasured shift would have to be to explain the estimate away (Cinelli and Hazlett, 2020). The line starts with Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983), who parameterised an unobserved binary confounder by its association with treatment and outcome, and continues with Imbens (2003), who gave the partial-$R^2$ parameterisation in the regression setting. Alternative summaries are the E-value (VanderWeele and Ding (2017)), the coefficient-stability bound of Altonji et al. (2005) as generalised by Oster (2019), and the distributional (selection-model) approach of Tan (2006). Masten and Poirier (2018) study identification when the independence assumption is only partially imposed. For structural causal models, Cinelli et al. (2019) extend sensitivity analysis beyond a single confounder. We adapt this literature to the HMM-regime channel as a one-parameter *robustness value* $b^\star$.

## 2.8 Air-quality attribution and its causal methodology

This is the applied literature that the data and the question belong to. It splits into five groups.

**Exposure and health effects.** The classical cohort studies (Dockery et al. (1993); Pope III (2002)) established associations between fine particulate matter and mortality; Dominici et al. (2002) estimated regional and national dose-response relationships with time-series models. Zigler and Dominici (2014) argued that air-pollution epidemiology should move from exposure-response estimation to policy-relevant potential-outcomes questions, and Wu et al. (2020) applied doubly robust and propensity-score methods to long-term PM$_{2.5}$ exposure and mortality in a national cohort. Henneman et al. (2019) link reductions in coal power-plant emissions to improvements in health outcomes ("accountability" analysis). These studies work with *annual* or *daily* data, health outcomes and rich covariates; they do not address hour-scale pollutant-to-pollutant dynamics.

**Burden and trends.** Global and national burden estimates (Cohen et al. (2017); Burnett et al. (2018); Lelieveld et al. (2015)) and the India-specific state-level analysis of Balakrishnan et al. (2019) motivate the policy interest. Satellite-derived PM$_{2.5}$ surfaces (Hammer et al. (2020); van Donkelaar et al. (2021)) supply exposure maps where monitors are absent; they are spatial products and do not resolve hourly co-movement at a monitor.

**Evaluating interventions.** The dominant causal tool in the atmospheric literature is *meteorological normalisation*: a machine-learning model of pollutant concentration on weather and calendar variables is fitted, and the effect of an intervention is read from the change in the weather-normalised series. Grange et al. (2018) developed the random-forest version for Swiss PM$_{10}$, Grange and Carslaw (2019) formalised its use for detecting interventions, Vu et al. (2019) applied it to the Beijing Clean Air Action, and Shi et al. (2021) to COVID-19 lockdowns in eleven cities. Zhang et al. (2019) attribute the PM$_{2.5}$ decline in China (2013–2017) to emission controls using chemical-transport modelling. For India, regulation and policy have been evaluated by quasi-experimental designs (Greenstone and Hanna (2014)), and the emission inventory of coal-fired plants is documented in Guttikunda and Jawahar (2014). These designs require an *event* (a policy date, a lockdown); the question studied here, how much a continuous change in NO$_2$ moves PM$_{2.5}$, has none.

**Data and monitoring in India.** Pant et al. (2019) review particulate monitoring in India and its limits (coverage, calibration, data gaps); Gani et al. (2019) characterise submicron aerosol composition in Delhi and show how strongly secondary aerosol and local combustion vary by season; satellite NO$_2$ studies (Beirle et al. (2011)) quantify nitrogen-oxide emissions and lifetimes from above. These motivate our data-quality screens (Chapters 6 and 8) and our treatment of NO$_2$ as one of several co-varying combustion tracers.

**Source apportionment and tooling.** Receptor-model apportionment (positive matrix factorisation) attributes ambient particulate matter to source categories using chemical speciation; it needs speciated filter data that the CPCB hourly feed does not contain. The `openair` toolkit (Carslaw and Ropkins (2012)) is the standard open implementation of the diagnostics and normalisation used in the literature above. Fann et al. (2012) show how modelled concentration changes are translated into avoided health burden, which is the downstream use of an estimate like ours.

**Positioning.** To our knowledge, the closest atmospheric methods are the meteorological-normalisation family: they treat weather as the confounder to be removed, with an event as the identifying contrast. The regime view adds a second, *unmeasured* source of co-movement (stagnation episodes that persist for hours to days and are visible only through meteorology), and asks what happens when that source is estimated rather than observed. We make no claim that NO$_2$ causes PM$_{2.5}$ in the atmosphere; Chapter 11 states that the estimates are conditional associations whose bias we can characterise but not eliminate.

The gap is therefore not "another estimator", but a quantitative account of what a generated state does to the downstream estimating equation, together with the estimator and reporting rules that account implies. Concretely, the survey leaves four questions open, and each maps to a contribution of this project:

1. *Dependent data with a latent confounder.* DML guarantees need observed confounders, and deconfounder guarantees need identification. What is the exact bias of the plug-in posterior estimator on a dependent series? (Chapter 5.)
2. *Diagnostics that point the wrong way.* Overlap and conditioning diagnostics are validated on observed covariates. Do they remain trustworthy when the covariate is generated? (Chapters 5 and 7.)
3. *Reporting.* Sensitivity analysis exists for a static confounder; what is the one-parameter summary for a regime channel? (Chapters 5 and 8, $b^\star$.)
4. *Applied evidence without an event.* Atmospheric causal studies rely on interventions; what can be said, and with what honesty, for hourly pollutant pairs in Indian cities that have none? (Chapters 8 and 11.)
