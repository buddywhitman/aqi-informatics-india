Executive Summary

This project builds a small, reproducible data-science investigation to screen hourly air-pollution observations for unusual patterns. It asks whether a high reading is consistent with recent conditions, the second pollutant, and available weather context. The result is an interpretable AQI Suspicion Score with Normal, Watch, and Suspicious labels; it is a review aid, not a verdict about whether a sensor reading is true or false.

The analysis uses real CPCB station readings distributed by the India Air Quality Database (XKDR Forum), paired by local hour with Open-Meteo historical weather. The selected slice contains 701 complete PM2.5 and NO2 observations from Alipur, Delhi, during November 2024. Weather was available for all 701 observation hours. The reported status counts are 666 Normal, 35 Watch, and 0 Suspicious. The highest score is 45/100, assigned to several hours during a very high PM2.5 episode because the index is extreme relative to this month and PM2.5 differs strongly from NO2.

The principal result is methodological: the supplied project formula is transparent and easy to explain, but the 100% relative-jump condition did not occur in this month after standardizing pollutants and rescaling them to a 0–500 proxy. The project therefore exposes a threshold-calibration issue rather than manufacturing a Suspicious case. It also demonstrates why analysts must inspect score components and retain the raw pollutant observations.

1. Introduction

Air-quality monitoring systems compress complex environmental conditions into a set of measurements. A sharp rise may reflect a genuine pollution event, local accumulation, a short-lived emissions source, instrument behaviour, a data-transfer problem, or a combination of causes. A single pollutant value cannot distinguish among these explanations. A simple data-quality screen can help identify observations that merit inspection by a human analyst.

This mini-project follows the workflow described in the supplied aqi-informatics-india project material: grouped time-series processing, lags and rolling statistics, city-relative pollutant standardization, weather context, an interpretable score, and interactive visual analysis. The analysis is intentionally narrower than a multi-city research pipeline so the data source, score, and limitations can be explained and reproduced in a classroom setting.

2. Problem Statement

For each observed hour at one Delhi monitoring station, calculate how unusual the current pollution pattern is compared with the recent six-hour baseline; compare PM2.5 and NO2 behaviour; and check whether low wind makes accumulation more plausible. Classify each hour as Normal, Watch, or Suspicious. The project does not estimate future AQI and does not claim to diagnose faults or identify pollution causes.

3. Data and Dataset Use

The project uses two real-world data sources. Air-quality readings are hourly measurements from the CPCB Continuous Ambient Air Quality Monitoring network, distributed through the XKDR Forum India Air Quality Database. The dataset is not dummy or simulated: each row originates from a monitoring station record. For a compact reproducible demonstration, the script queries station site_5024 (Alipur, Delhi – DPCC) for November 2024 and the PM2.5 and NO2 parameters. The public demo key is documented by the database and is limited to 2024 and 10,000 rows per query.

Weather is historical ERA5 reanalysis provided through Open-Meteo for the station coordinates and the same dates, in Asia/Kolkata time. ERA5 combines observations with a numerical weather model to provide a spatially complete estimate; these weather values are not measurements from the air-quality monitor itself. The nearest grid-cell series is joined to each pollutant record by timestamp.

Data preparation and scope

The selected month has 744 possible hourly slots. The source returned 701 hours with both pollutants, or 94.2% coverage. Missing hours are left missing; the script does not fill them with synthetic values. This avoids creating artificial spikes or smooth trends. The analysis is station-specific and month-specific, so city-relative statistics mean “relative to Alipur during this selected month,” not a long-term Delhi norm.

The source API reports the selected station’s timestamps in Indian Standard Time. The weather request uses Asia/Kolkata so the join is aligned to local time. The script checks the required air-data columns, coerces pollutant and weather values to numeric types, aligns the hourly series, and saves both downloaded source files for reproducibility.

4. Methodology

The workflow turns the source readings into a sequence of transparent features. It uses pandas for tabular cleaning and grouped rolling calculations, NumPy for numeric score operations, and Plotly when installed for interactive figures. The supplied script also creates static PNG charts when Plotly is unavailable.

Why a six-hour rolling median?

A rolling median resists the influence of one very high or low observation. The calculation uses shift(1), so the hour being evaluated is excluded from its own baseline. The first two hours do not have the minimum three prior observations needed for the rolling features; those rows remain in the output, and their jump flags are off because their jump values are unavailable.

The wind feature is deliberately modest. Low wind can reduce near-surface dispersion and make pollutant accumulation more plausible, but wind alone cannot establish the cause of an event. The feature is used as supporting context, not as a causal claim.

5. Features and AQI Suspicion Score

The script uses a deterministic weighted rule rather than fitting a black-box classifier. This is appropriate for a classroom screen because the user can explain exactly why a row receives points. The output is an AQI Suspicion Score (0–100) even though the underlying Pollution Severity Index is not an official AQI.

Interpretation safeguard

A high score indicates unusualness under the chosen rules; it does not prove sensor error, deliberate manipulation, or the source of emissions. A true pollution episode can be unusual and still be valid. Every flagged row must be checked against station metadata, neighbouring monitors, instrument logs, and the original provider before operational use.

6. Python Code and How to Run It

The companion file pollution_spike_investigation.py is a single executable script. It downloads the real source slice by default, runs cleaning and feature engineering, prints a summary, and saves the scored data and three charts. No separate dataset download is required when the machine has internet access.

Run steps

Install Python 3.10 or later.

Install dependencies: pip install pandas numpy matplotlib plotly

Run: python pollution_spike_investigation.py

Open outputs/pollution_timeline.html, outputs/severity_vs_suspicion.html, and outputs/pollution_weather_context.html to present the interactive graphs. If Plotly is absent, static PNG files are produced instead.

The raw air CSV and weather JSON are cached in outputs/raw_data; scored observations, case table and run summary are also saved in outputs.

Core analysis sequence

pollutant severity = (value − station median) / station IQR

pollution proxy = clip(100 + 25 × mean(PM2.5 severity, NO2 severity), 0, 500)

recent baseline = median of up to six prior hourly proxy values

jump ratio = absolute deviation from recent baseline / (recent baseline + 1)

score = weighted sum of extreme, jump, wind-context and pollutant-disagreement flags

The script writes hourly_scored_observations.csv with all raw fields, derived features, score components, and class labels; suspicious_cases.csv with the highest Suspicious rows (empty in this run); and run_summary.json with counts and the top scored case. If the professor requests an offline run, the script accepts --air-csv and --weather-json paths for the already-fetched real source files.

Reproducible offline example: python pollution_spike_investigation.py --air-csv outputs/raw_data/delhi_cpcb_hourly_nov2024.csv --weather-json outputs/raw_data/delhi_weather_nov2024.json

What this case suggests

The high PM2.5 values and strong pollutant disagreement merit review against nearby stations and instrument records. They do not by themselves indicate a bad reading: particulate matter and NO2 can respond differently to emissions, weather, source mix and station location. The plot also makes clear that a top-period value is not automatically a suspicious observation under the scoring rule.

Weather Context, Limitations and Discussion

Figure 3. Pollution Severity Index proxy and Open-Meteo ERA5 wind speed (km/h), joined by IST hour.

Wind context is shown alongside the pollution proxy because low wind can be compatible with local accumulation. The weather values are gridded reanalysis estimates rather than readings at the DPCC monitor. The code also retains temperature, relative humidity and surface pressure, but they are not included in the weighted score. This prevents the project from implying that it has built a complete atmospheric model.

Limitations and risks to interpretation

Single station and one month: station-period medians and IQRs are not long-term or city-wide normal conditions.

Non-regulatory index: the Pollution Severity Index is a standardized comparison aid. It is not the Indian National AQI and must not be used for health advice or regulatory reporting.

Threshold sensitivity: the 100% relative-jump threshold operates on a rescaled index. In this run, the proxy stayed between 66.4 and 163.8 and no jump exceeded the threshold; no Suspicious class was produced. Thresholds should be evaluated against labelled historical episodes and neighbouring monitors before deployment.

Weather uncertainty: ERA5 reanalysis is spatially gridded and model-assisted. The low-wind check is exploratory and cannot prove causality.

Provider and instrument uncertainty: monitoring records can have gaps, calibration issues or preliminary values. The screening score does not replace source validation or instrument logs.

Recommended next analysis

For a stronger follow-up, extend the same workflow across multiple Delhi stations and several seasons; compare synchronized readings from nearby monitors; calibrate thresholds using documented maintenance events and known pollution episodes; and report sensitivity and false-positive rates. Keep the simple score as an interpretable baseline while validating whether it improves review prioritization.

8. Conclusion

This project presents an end-to-end, real-data screening workflow for hourly pollution spikes. It obtains CPCB-derived PM2.5 and NO2 readings, aligns them with historical weather, builds city-relative pollutant features and a robust recent baseline, calculates an interpretable score, and saves graphs and case tables for review.

In the Alipur November 2024 run, 35 of 701 observations were labeled Watch and none were labeled Suspicious. The highest score was 45/100. The mid-November PM2.5 rise is visible in the timeline and deserves contextual review, while the absence of Suspicious labels reveals that the supplied relative-jump threshold is poorly matched to the standardized index for this sample. The next step is not to force a higher label; it is to validate thresholds against multiple stations, longer history and trusted episode labels.

The central conclusion is that an extreme concentration and a suspicious measurement are different questions. This project helps identify observations that deserve a second look; it cannot decide whether a reading is true, false, or caused by a specific source.
